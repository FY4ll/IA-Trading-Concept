# Market microstructure vs. retail "levels" lore: round numbers, order clustering, stop cascades, liquidity, support/resistance (FX, gold, indices, oil, crypto)

Scope reminder: COLLAPSAR trades FTMO CFDs (FX, metals, indices, energies, crypto), horizons 15 min to a few weeks, flat before weekends, and sees only OHLC + tick volume (no order book, no signed order flow). Every number below comes from the cited source unless marked "(from memory, not re-verified)". Primary PDFs of Osler's three FRBNY papers were downloaded and read in full text for this note.

---

## 1. Carol Osler (FRBNY): what her three papers actually show

### Takeaway
Osler's work is the only solid empirical bridge between "support/resistance" lore and real order data. It shows (a) customer take-profit and stop-loss orders at one big FX bank are heavily clustered at round numbers, take-profits ON the round number, stop-losses JUST BEYOND it; (b) published support/resistance levels and round numbers "stop" intraday FX trends more often than random levels, but only by about 4-5 percentage points over a base rate that is already about 55 %; (c) after a round number is crossed, moves are faster (stop-loss "cascades"), but the extra move is tiny (thousandths of a percent at 15 min) and fades within hours, not days. Real, statistically strong, economically very small, and measured on 1996-2000 data.

### Cited Findings

**Osler (2000), "Support for Resistance: Technical Analysis and Intraday Exchange Rates", FRBNY Economic Policy Review, July 2000 (vol. 6 no. 2), pp. 53-68**
- Data: support/resistance levels distributed daily to customers by six firms active in FX, for dollar-mark, dollar-yen and dollar-pound, January 1996 to April 1998, tested against minute-by-minute quotes during New York hours (dates and pairs restated in Osler's 2001 staff report) — [FRBNY EPR article page](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/epr/00v06n2/0007osle.html); [Osler SR125 p.2-3](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr125.pdf)
- Context she cites: 25-30 % of FX traders base most of their trades on technical signals (Cheung & Chinn 1999; Cheung & Wong 1999); technical analysis is a primary or secondary information source for more than 90 % of FX participants in London (Allen & Taylor 1992) and Hong Kong (Lui & Mole 1998) — [Osler 2000 PDF](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/epr/00v06n2/0007osle.pdf)
- Published levels are mostly round numbers: more than 70 % end in 0 and 96 % end in 0 or 5 (vs. 10 % / 20 % if random) — [Osler 2000 PDF](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/epr/00v06n2/0007osle.pdf)
- Method: a "hit" = price comes within 0.01 % of the level (0.00 % and 0.02 % also tried); a "bounce" = price has not gone through the level 15 minutes later (30 min also tried); benchmark = 10,000 sets of artificial random levels; test = number of months (out of 28) in which published bounce frequency beats the artificial one (binomial) — [Osler 2000 PDF](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/epr/00v06n2/0007osle.pdf)
- Core result: rates bounced off artificial levels 56.2 % of the time vs. 60.8 % at published levels (Table 8). In all 16 firm-currency pairs, published levels beat artificial ones; significant at 5 % for all but three pairs. Average edge: +4.2 pp (mark), +5.6 pp (yen), +4.0 pp (pound). Best case: Firm 1 on yen, 66.5 % vs 57.3 % (+9.2 pp). Gap between best and worst firm ≈ 4.0 pp — [Osler 2000 PDF](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/epr/00v06n2/0007osle.pdf)
- Note the base rate: with the exact-hit definition, bounce frequencies at random levels "fall slightly short of 50 percent"; with the 0.01 % proximity window used, random levels already "bounce" about 55-57 % of the time — [Osler 2000 PDF](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/epr/00v06n2/0007osle.pdf)
- Regime dependence: in the first (low-volatility) half of the sample the effect was significant in all but one case; in the second half (normal volatility) published levels still did better but were no longer significant in half the cases — [Osler 2000 PDF](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/epr/00v06n2/0007osle.pdf)
- Persistence: five days after publication, levels still beat random levels for all firms and currencies (significant in 9 of 16 cases), with bounce frequency 1.7 pp lower than on publication day — [Osler 2000 PDF](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/epr/00v06n2/0007osle.pdf)
- "Agreed" levels (published by more than one firm) had bounce frequencies "quite close" to single-firm levels: consensus adds little — [Osler 2000 PDF](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/epr/00v06n2/0007osle.pdf)

**Osler (2003), "Currency Orders and Exchange Rate Dynamics: An Explanation for the Predictive Success of Technical Analysis", Journal of Finance 58(5):1791-1819 (volume/pages from memory, not re-verified); working version FRBNY Staff Report 125 (2001)**
- Data: almost 9,700 stop-loss and take-profit orders (exact: 9,667) at a large dealing bank, 1 Sept 1999 to 11 April 2000; 43 % dollar-yen, 33 % euro-dollar, 24 % dollar-pound; mean order $5.8 M, median $3.0 M; ~92 new orders/day; median order life 0.4 days (mean 3.4 days); >70 % deleted or closed within one business day; about one third executed; conditional orders informally ≈ 5-10 % of the bank's total deal flow — [Osler SR125](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr125.pdf)
- (Discrepancy to flag: the 2002 cascades draft describes the same data set as the complete order book of the Royal Bank of Scotland, 1 August 1999 - 11 April 2000, 9,655 orders, aggregate face value over $55 billion — [Osler SR150](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr150.pdf))
- Clustering: under no clustering each last-two-digit ending would get ≈1 %; uniformity rejected far beyond the 0.01 % level (Anderson-Darling). Biggest cluster at endings 00 (≈ 8.7 % of orders), then 50, then other 0 endings, then 5; no special cluster at 25/75 — [Osler SR125](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr125.pdf)
- Take-profit vs stop-loss: 9.3 % of take-profit orders sit exactly at 00 vs only 4.4 % of stop-loss orders; take-profits significantly more clustered at round numbers (marginal significance 1.1 %) — [Osler SR125](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr125.pdf)
- Stop-loss buy orders cluster just ABOVE round numbers, stop-loss sells just BELOW: 7.4 % of stop-loss buys at endings 90-99 vs 14.4 % at 01-10 (SR125); in the SR150 version, 14.3 % of executed stop-loss buys end in [01,10] vs 6.9 % in [90,99]; 9.9 % of executed take-profits (value-weighted) end exactly in 00 vs 3.8 % for stop-losses — [Osler SR125](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr125.pdf); [Osler SR150](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr150.pdf)
- Interpretation in the paper: take-profit clusters at the round number make it a "partially reflecting barrier" (reversal), stop-loss clusters just beyond make crossings accelerate (continuation). In her simulations the round-number bounce-frequency gain averages ≈ 3.4 pp vs ≈ 4.6 pp observed in real rates (mark, yen, pound) — [Osler SR125](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr125.pdf)
- A dealer's own conditional-order book is private information that can be used for risk management (example: with a large take-profit sell at 1.6010 and spot at 1.6000, the dealer can go short "with relative safety"); exchange rates tend to spend LESS time at round numbers than at other rates (De Grauwe & Decupere 1992) — [Osler SR125](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr125.pdf)

**Osler (2005), "Stop-loss orders and price cascades in currency markets", Journal of International Money and Finance 24(2):219-241; working version FRBNY Staff Report 150 (2002)**
- Publication details — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/fip/fednsr/150.html)
- Three results: (1) trends are unusually rapid when rates reach levels where stop-losses cluster; (2) the response to stop-loss orders is larger than to take-profits; (3) it lasts longer. "Most results are statistically significant for hours, although not for days" — [Osler SR150 abstract](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr150.pdf)
- Price data: minute quotes for dollar-mark, dollar-yen, dollar-pound split into 58 intervals of 10 trading days (consistent with Jan 1996-Apr 1998 NY hours); round numbers = endings 00 and 50; "reach" = within 0.01 %; "cross" = beyond the level 15 minutes later; bootstrap vs 10,000 sets of 30 arbitrary levels — [Osler SR150](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr150.pdf)
- Size of the acceleration: dollar-mark moves on average 0.061 % in the 15 min after crossing a round number vs 0.054 % after crossing an arbitrary level (difference ≈ 0.007 %), round > arbitrary in 51 of 58 intervals (p < 0.001 %) — [Osler SR150](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr150.pdf)
- Reversal at round numbers (take-profit effect): dollar-mark reverses 59.3 % at round numbers vs 54.8 % at arbitrary ones (46 of 58 intervals); significant for mark and yen, NOT for pound — [Osler SR150](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr150.pdf)
- Crossing vs reversing: dollar-yen moves 0.0130 % more (15 min) after crossing than after reversing at a round number; difference stays positive at 30 min, 1 h and 2 h for all three pairs, and at 1 day only for yen — [Osler SR150](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr150.pdf)
- Very large orders (≥ $50 M): largest stop-loss ≈ $450 M vs largest take-profit < $200 M; > 62 % of the face value of very large stop-losses sits within [90,09] of a 00 level vs 28 % for very large take-profits; very large stop-losses sit on average 4.25 % from spot vs 2.25 % for take-profits (stops are far away, so they matter in big moves/crises) — [Osler SR150](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr150.pdf)
- Motivation: since 1990 dollar-yen moves above 4 standard deviations occurred 85x more often than a normal distribution predicts; the paper proposes stop cascades as one contributor to these "fat tails"; cascades "of varying sizes, might happen as frequently as once per week if rates are particularly volatile" (stated as possibility, not measured) — [Osler SR150](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr150.pdf)

### Inferences
- The effect sizes are below retail trading costs. A 0.007 % extra move is about 0.08 pip on EURUSD at 1.10 and far below a typical CFD spread + commission (≈ 0.005-0.01 % on majors). A 4-5 pp improvement in "bounce" probability over a 55 % base, measured 15 minutes after the touch, does not by itself make a profitable trade after costs. Osler herself never claims a tradable strategy.
- The base rate is the key lesson for COLLAPSAR: random levels already "hold" about 55 % of the time at a 15-minute horizon. Any chart anecdote "price reacted at the level" must be compared to that base rate.
- The documented mechanism is round numbers in the market's quoting convention (00/50), not swing highs/lows, "order blocks" or trend-line levels. Stops cluster just beyond round numbers, so a break of a round number tends to continue a little (minutes to hours), not to "sweep and reverse".
- The data are 1996-2000 voice/early-electronic bank-customer flow. Today's FX is dominated by algorithmic and electronic trading (see §4); no public study replicates Osler's order-book result on recent data (see Gaps). An independent non-academic test on 2018-2026 hourly FX found no round-number effect (see §2), consistent with decay.
- For the AI: "distance to the nearest 00/50 level" is a legitimate, cheap feature to show (it is information, not a trigger), but expected value from it alone should be assumed near zero after costs.

### Gaps
- Exact published-version numbers in the Journal of Finance (2003) and JIMF (2005) were not re-read; the figures above come from the FRBNY staff-report versions (2001-2002), which may differ slightly from the final articles.
- No study found replicating Osler's order-book clustering with post-2010 data (e.g., EBS/Refinitiv order books, retail broker books). Unknown whether the stop-just-beyond pattern persists in today's algorithmic market.
- Osler did not test gold, indices, oil or crypto.

## 2. Round-number effects and price clustering in other markets (stocks, gold, crypto)

### Takeaway
Price clustering at round numbers is one of the most robust facts in market microstructure (stocks, futures, gold, Bitcoin). But clustering of prices or orders does not automatically mean predictable returns: some studies find small barrier effects (gold at $100 levels, Dutch stocks), the largest stock study finds a small, cost-sized behavioral imbalance around integers, and the Bitcoin study finds clustering with "no significant pattern of returns after the round number".

### Cited Findings
- Harris (1991), "Stock Price Clustering and Discreteness", Review of Financial Studies 4(3):389-415: stock prices cluster on round fractions; clustering increases with price level and volatility and decreases with capitalization and trading frequency; explained by traders using coarser price sets "to simplify negotiations" — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v4y1991i3p389-415.html); [paper PDF](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~keechung/MGF743/Readings/Stock%20price%20clustering%20and%20price%20discreteness.pdf)
- Osler notes the parallel: within whole numbers, stock prices cluster at endings 10 and 5, stronger at 10 (Harris 1991); clustering stronger in dealer markets than exchanges — [Osler SR125](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr125.pdf)
- Mitchell, J. (2001), "Clustering and psychological barriers: the importance of numbers", Journal of Futures Markets 21(5):395-428 (IDEAS lists R. I. Webb and J. Mitchell): survey arguing clustering derives "from a variety of sources" (number preference, decimal rounding, negotiation), not from mathematical properties of numbers — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/jfutmk/v21y2001i5p395-428.html)
- Sonnemans (2006), "Price clustering and natural resistance points in the Dutch stock market: A natural experiment", European Economic Review 50(8):1937-1950: Dutch stocks 1990-2001; prices cluster at round numbers and pass round numbers less often than other numbers ("barriers"). Natural experiment = switch to euro quotes on 1 Jan 1999 while guilders stayed in daily life until 2002: round-number effects changed abruptly with the quoting currency (supports the "odd price"/quoting hypothesis, against slowly changing "aspiration levels") — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/eecrev/v50y2006i8p1937-1950.html); [Tinbergen](https://tinbergen.nl/publication/159150/price-clustering-and-natural-resistance-points-in-the-dutch-stock-market-a-natural-experiment)
- Bhattacharya, Holden & Jacobsen (2012), "Penny Wise, Dollar Foolish: Buy-Sell Imbalances On and Around Round Numbers", Management Science 58(2):413-431: random sample of > 100 million US stock trades; excess buying by liquidity demanders one cent below round numbers and excess selling one cent above; imbalance monotonic in "roundness" (integers > half-dollars > ...); much stronger when the ask falls to reach the integer than when price crosses it; these trades cost liquidity demanders losses "approaching $1 billion per year" — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/ab_id=1569922); [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v58y2012i2p413-431.html); [authors' PDF](https://host.kelley.iu.edu/cholden/Bhattacharya%20Holden%20and%20Jacobsen%20(2012).pdf)
- Gold, peer-reviewed: Aggarwal & Lucey (2007), "Psychological barriers in gold prices?", Review of Financial Economics 16(2):217-230: daily and intraday gold series; round numbers act as barriers affecting the conditional mean and variance; at the $100 levels gold is less likely to continue its path, and volatility changes near/just after a barrier, especially when falling — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/revfin/v16y2007i2p217-230.html); [TCD working paper](https://www.tcd.ie/triss/assets/PDFs/iiis/iiisdp53.pdf)
- Osler also notes clustering of actual prices observed in the London gold market (footnote 14) — [Osler SR125](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr125.pdf)
- Gold, NON-peer-reviewed (blog by a practitioner, M. D. Ede, MQL5): XAUUSD hourly, one broker, March 2018-September 2026, 50,000 bars ($1,166-$5,563). Approaching a $20 level with momentum: carried through 49.2 % (rising) / 45.2 % (falling) vs ≈ 49.7 % / 47.0 % at control levels; mean "rejection" ≈ $0.33-0.38; phase-rotation null z = -2.64; influence ≈ $1 wide (gone by $3); gold crosses a $20 level 3.9x per day and 18.9 % of hours are within $2 of one. Effect ≈ $0.33 vs spreads $0.20-0.35 → "roughly break-even before slippage". Same test on GBPUSD, EURUSD, USDJPY, AUDUSD: nothing (all p > 0.24) — [MQL5 blog](https://www.mql5.com/en/blogs/post/775454) (UNVERIFIED: single author, single broker feed, no peer review)
- Crypto: Urquhart (2017), "Price clustering in Bitcoin", Economics Letters 159:145-148: over 10 % of prices end in 00 decimals, "but there is no significant pattern of returns after the round number"; clustering rises with price and volume (supports negotiation hypothesis) — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ecolet/v159y2017icp145-148.html); [Southampton eprints](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/412873)
- Follow-ups exist on Bitcoin clustering (day-of-week, sentiment, extension in Finance Research Letters 2019/2020) — [Finance Research Letters 2019](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finlet/v29y2019icp111-116.html); [FRL 2020 extension](https://ideas.repec.org:443/a/eee/finlet/v32y2020ics1544612318305907.html) (contents not reviewed)

### Inferences
- Clustering is a fact about WHERE people put prices and orders; profitability requires that price paths around those numbers differ from chance by more than costs. Across the cited evidence, when a return effect exists it is of the order of the spread (gold $20 levels; BHJ stock imbalances; Osler FX).
- The effect seems market- and era-specific: present in gold (2007 paper; 2018-2026 blog), absent in recent hourly FX majors (blog), absent in returns for Bitcoin (Urquhart). This argues for letting the AI see "distance to round numbers" per market and learn whether it matters, rather than hard-coding any level logic.
- Indices and oil: no specific peer-reviewed round-number study was retrieved here; the stock-market and futures literature (Harris, Sonnemans, Mitchell) suggests clustering exists at index round thousands/hundreds and oil whole dollars, but return effects are unmeasured in this note.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed round-number return study for equity-index CFDs (US500, US30, DAX) or crude oil was found in this search pass.
- Effect sizes from Aggarwal & Lucey (2007) (exact probabilities, basis points) were not retrieved; only the qualitative result.
- The 2018-2026 gold and FX result is from an unreviewed blog; treat as a hypothesis COLLAPSAR could test on its own Dukascopy data.

## 3. Is technical analysis profitable at all? (BLL 1992, Lo-Mamaysky-Wang 2000, Park & Irwin 2007, Neely-Weller-Ulrich 2009, Menkhoff & Taylor 2007)

### Takeaway
The academic record says: simple technical rules had genuine excess returns in older data (stocks before ~1987, FX in the 1970s-80s), but these largely disappeared once published and once data-snooping was properly controlled; patterns can carry some statistical information without delivering profits after costs. Technical analysis remains massively USED by FX professionals, which partly explains why order-based effects (Osler) exist at all.

### Cited Findings
- Brock, Lakonishok & LeBaron (1992), "Simple Technical Trading Rules and the Stochastic Properties of Stock Returns", Journal of Finance 47(5):1731-1764: moving-average and trading-range-break rules on the Dow Jones 1897-1986; buy signals earned higher, less volatile returns than sell signals; returns after sell signals were negative; results not explained by random walk, AR(1), GARCH-M or EGARCH null models — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v47y1992i5p1731-64.html)
- Sullivan, Timmermann & White (1999), "Data-Snooping, Technical Trading Rule Performance, and the Bootstrap", Journal of Finance 54:1647-1691: White's Reality Check over an expanded universe of BLL-type rules on 100 years of DJIA; finding (as summarized by the source): no significantly profitable rule for the DJIA, S&P 500 index and S&P 500 futures in the later sample — [summary](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/361689); [LSE repository](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/119144). (From memory, not re-verified: the best BLL-universe rule survived the Reality Check in the original 1897-1986 sample but did not outperform out-of-sample 1987-1996.)
- Lo, Mamaysky & Wang (2000), "Foundations of Technical Analysis", Journal of Finance 55:1705-1765: automatic pattern recognition by kernel regression (head-and-shoulders, double bottoms, etc.) on many US stocks 1962-1996; conditional return distributions after several patterns differ from unconditional ones, so "several technical indicators do provide incremental information and may have some practical value" — [MIT page](https://web.mit.edu/Alo/www/Papers/techanal.html); [DSpace@MIT](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/28725). Note: "incremental information" = distributional differences, not demonstrated net trading profits.
- Park & Irwin (2007), "What Do We Know About the Profitability of Technical Analysis?", Journal of Economic Surveys 21(4):786-826: of 92 "modern" studies, 58 positive, 24 negative, 10 mixed — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jecsur/v21y2007i4p786-826.html); [AgEcon working paper](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/37487/files/AgMAS04_04.pdf). (From memory, not re-verified: the authors stress that many positive studies suffer from data snooping, ex-post rule selection and poor estimation of risk and transaction costs.)
- Neely, Weller & Ulrich (2009), "The Adaptive Markets Hypothesis: Evidence from the Foreign Exchange Market", JFQA 44(2):467-488: FX technical excess returns of the 1970s-80s were genuine (not data mining) but had disappeared by the early 1990s for filter and moving-average rules; returns to less-studied rules also declined but "have probably not completely disappeared" — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v44y2009i02p467-488_09.html); [St. Louis Fed WP](https://files.stlouisfed.org/files/htdocs/wp/2006/2006-046.pdf)
- Menkhoff & Taylor (2007), "The Obstinate Passion of Foreign Exchange Professionals: Technical Analysis", Journal of Economic Literature 45(4):936-972 — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/wrk/warwec/769.html) (full text not retrieved; see Gaps)
- Usage figures (survey-based, as cited by Osler 2000): >90 % of London FX participants use technical analysis as primary or secondary input (Allen & Taylor 1992); 25-30 % of FX traders base most trades on it (Cheung & Chinn 1999) — [Osler 2000 PDF](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/epr/00v06n2/0007osle.pdf)
- Osler (2001) summarizes the older FX literature as "virtually all" finding predictive power for technical rules (filter rules, Levich & Thomas 1993, head-and-shoulders in Chang & Osler 1999, genetic algorithms in Neely et al. 1997) — [Osler SR125](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr125.pdf). This is pre-2000 evidence and is superseded by the decay documented in Neely-Weller-Ulrich (2009).

### Inferences
- The decay pattern (real edges in the 1970s-80s, gone after publication/automation) matches the project's own finding that fixed exam periods produce period-specific AIs: whatever COLLAPSAR learns from price shapes should be tested on rolling, unseen periods and expected to decay.
- "Patterns carry information" (Lo et al.) is weaker than "patterns pay after costs". A learning AI that sees raw prices can in principle discover any of these patterns; there is no academic reason to inject named patterns (head-and-shoulders, order blocks) as hand-made features.

### Gaps
- Menkhoff & Taylor (2007) exact survey numbers and conclusions (e.g., share of professionals using TA by horizon, their explanations for profitability) were not retrieved.
- Exact BLL return figures (annualized buy vs sell returns) were not verified in this pass.
- No retrieved study measures TA profitability specifically on CFD-traded crude oil, gold or index CFDs at 15-min to multi-week horizons after realistic retail costs.

## 4. Microstructure theory and order flow: what moves prices, and can a retail CFD trader see it?

### Takeaway
Theory (Kyle 1985; Glosten & Milgrom 1985) and evidence (Evans & Lyons 2002) agree that signed order flow, not public news, explains most short-run FX moves: daily interdealer order flow explained more than 60 % of daily DM/$ changes. But this flow is contemporaneous, comes from interdealer data that retail traders cannot see, and spot FX is decentralized: a retail feed's "volume" is a count of quote updates (ticks) at one broker, not traded volume or buy/sell direction.

### Cited Findings
- Kyle (1985), "Continuous Auctions and Insider Trading", Econometrica 53(6):1315-1335: an informed trader hides among noise traders; a market maker sets price as a linear function of total order flow (price impact "lambda"); information is incorporated gradually — [DOI 10.2307/1913210](https://doi.org/10.2307/1913210) (bibliographic details from memory, not re-verified)
- Glosten & Milgrom (1985), "Bid, ask and transaction prices in a specialist market with heterogeneously informed traders", Journal of Financial Economics 14(1):71-100: the bid-ask spread exists even with zero costs because the market maker loses to informed traders and recoups from uninformed ones; each trade moves the quote — [DOI 10.1016/0304-405X(85)90044-3](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(85)90044-3) (bibliographic details from memory, not re-verified)
- Evans & Lyons (2002), "Order Flow and Exchange Rate Dynamics", Journal of Political Economy (Feb 2002): a model with interdealer order flow explains daily DM/$ log changes with R² above 60 %; $1 billion of net dollar purchases raises the DM price of the dollar by about 0.5 % — [NBER w7317](https://nber.org/papers/w7317); [JPE PDF](https://www.johnhcochrane.com/s/evans_lyons_jpe.pdf). (From memory, not re-verified: data were Reuters D2000-1 interdealer trades over 4 months in 1996; the relation is contemporaneous, not a forecast.)
- Osler (2002) quoting Evans (2001): "public news is rarely the predominant source of exchange rate movements over any horizon"; Cai et al. (2002): news of only "secondary importance" for extreme yen volatility in 1998 — [Osler SR150](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr150.pdf)
- Osler finds exchange rates respond even to non-informative order flow (take-profit clusters), consistent with downward-sloping demand / inventory effects rather than pure information — [Osler SR150](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr150.pdf)
- Market size/structure: BIS Triennial Survey April 2025: global FX turnover $9.6 trillion/day, up 28 % from 2022; spot = 31 % of turnover (28 % in 2022); data from > 1,100 banks/dealers in 52 jurisdictions — [BIS press release](https://www.bis.org/press/p250930.htm); [BIS statistics](https://www.bis.org/statistics/rpfx25_fx.htm); spot turnover ≈ $2.58 trillion/day and hedge funds + prop firms $758 billion/day as reported by a trade-press summary — [The Full FX](https://thefullfx.com/fx-turnover-hits-9-6-trillion-in-bis-triennial-survey/) (secondary source)
- Retail "volume": in spot FX/CFDs, MT5 tick volume counts price updates in one broker's feed; a large and a small trade both count as one tick; different brokers' feeds differ. Practitioner (non-peer-reviewed) comparisons report correlations of tick volume with real futures volume of about 0.71 to about 0.9 — [EarnForex guide](https://www.earnforex.com/guides/tick-volume-in-forex); [practitioner study PDF](https://c.mql5.com/forextsd/forum/145/fxvolume_tick_volume_vs_real_volume_study_1.pdf) (UNVERIFIED, non-academic)

### Inferences
- What COLLAPSAR sees (OHLC + tick volume) is a proxy for ACTIVITY (how busy the market is), not for DIRECTION of order flow. The microstructure variable that explains FX moves (signed order flow) is invisible to it. Tick volume is still useful as a volatility/activity clock (sessions, news), which is consistent with the intraday-activity literature.
- Because order flow's explanatory power is contemporaneous, even perfect access would not imply forecasting power at 15 min to weeks; the "institutional order flow" that retail educators claim to read from candles is not observable in OHLC data.
- Retail CFD prices are derived from the broker's liquidity providers; there is no single "institutional level" visible in that feed beyond the public round numbers and recent highs/lows that everyone sees.

### Gaps
- Exact Evans & Lyons sample and out-of-sample forecasting results were not re-read (only the 60 % R² and 0.5 % per $1 bn figures were confirmed).
- No peer-reviewed study comparing MT5/retail tick volume with true traded volume was found; only practitioner material.
- Order-flow evidence for gold, indices and oil CFD underlyings (futures markets with real volume) was not covered here.

## 5. Stop/liquidation cascades, leverage and margin spirals (FX, crypto)

### Takeaway
Self-reinforcing "cascades" are real: forced or price-contingent selling (stop-losses, liquidations, margin calls) can turn a move into a crash. Theory (Brunnermeier & Pedersen 2009) explains liquidity spirals; Osler documents the small-scale FX version; crypto perpetual futures produced the largest recorded episode (about $19 billion liquidated on 10 October 2025). But cascades are hard to predict from price alone: the most recent study finds price-based early warnings in only 5 of 7 big Bitcoin cascades and none in sudden-shock events; the only consistent precursor was in taker order-flow variance, which OHLC does not show.

### Cited Findings
- Brunnermeier & Pedersen (2009), "Market Liquidity and Funding Liquidity", Review of Financial Studies 22(6) (volume/issue from memory): traders' ability to provide liquidity depends on funding; funding (capital, margins) depends on market liquidity; under some conditions margins are destabilizing and the two reinforce each other → "liquidity spirals". Margin spiral: falling dealer wealth lowers liquidity → higher margins → tighter funding. Loss spiral: funding shock → illiquidity → losses on existing positions → forced sales → further price drop — [NBER SI 2005 draft](https://users.nber.org/~confer/2005/si2005/ap/brunnermeier.pdf); [Princeton](https://markus.scholar.princeton.edu/document/98)
- FX: stop-loss orders are positive-feedback trading; Osler links them to fat tails (dollar-yen -11 % on 7 Oct 1998; > 3 % on 7 March 2002; 4-sigma days 85x more frequent than normal) and shows crossing-acceleration effects significant for hours, not days — [Osler SR150](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr150.pdf)
- Crypto: Garcia Seuma (arXiv 2607.27070, submitted 29 July 2026, preprint, not peer-reviewed), "Where does the criticality live? Early-warning signals are event-heterogeneous across seven crypto-perpetual liquidation cascades": seven Bitcoin perpetual cascades 2022-2025 incl. May 2022 (LUNA/UST), Nov 2022 (FTX), Aug 2024 (yen-carry unwind) and the record ≈ $19 billion event of 10 October 2025. Rolling variance and lag-1 autocorrelation tested in 39 configurations per variable: price shows "critical slowing down" in 5 of 7 events but no warning in two sudden tariff-shock cascades; leverage carried the warning in Oct 2025; a compression of taker order-flow variance was the only consistent precursor (placebo p ≈ 5e-6); single-event predictability claims are "fragile by construction"; cascades look like "discontinuous, shock-driven transitions rather than critical ones" — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27070); [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.27070)
- Perpetual futures design: small, frequent funding payments keep perpetuals tied to spot (Gornall, Rinaldi & Xiao); funding-rate theory (He et al. 2022; Angeris et al. 2023) — as summarized in [arXiv 2506.08573](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.08573) (not read in full)
- Industry claims (NOT academic, unverified): "persistently high positive funding indicates crowded longs ... often precedes sharp liquidation-driven reversals"; "when liquidation intensity exceeds 5 % of open interest, forced selling triggers more forced selling" — [Elliptic corpus](https://www.elliptic.co/corpus/gen-3794/market-mechanism/derivatives-funding-rate-dynamics.html); [Amberdata blog](https://blog.amberdata.io/leverage-liquidations-the-31b-deleveraging) (marketing/industry sources; thresholds not peer-reviewed)

### Inferences
- For an FTMO account that is flat before weekends, cascades matter mainly as RISK: gaps and fast moves through stops (slippage), especially in crypto CFDs and around round-number breaks. A stop just beyond a round number sits exactly where Osler finds other stops cluster, i.e. where fills can be worst.
- Crypto CFDs at FTMO are priced off spot/perp markets whose funding rates, open interest and liquidation data COLLAPSAR does not see; from OHLC alone, the AI can at most learn volatility-regime signals (which predicted 5 of 7 cascades in-sample in one preprint, and missed shock-driven ones).
- Direction after a cascade is not established by the evidence gathered here (see §6 for the general liquidity-provision reversal result in equities).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed paper with measured average post-liquidation returns (reversal vs continuation) for BTC/ETH at 15-min to multi-day horizons was retrieved.
- Exact funding-rate predictability numbers were not found in academic sources; only industry blogs.
- Oil and index "stop cascade" studies (e.g., flash crashes, 2020 oil) were not covered.

## 6. Short-term reversal after large moves = paid liquidity provision (Nagel 2012)

### Takeaway
After large, non-informative price pressure, prices partially revert; the return to buying recent losers / selling recent winners at short horizons is a payment for providing liquidity, and it is largest exactly when liquidity "evaporates" (high VIX, crises). This is the academically grounded version of "liquidity" talk: the edge belongs to whoever has capital and nerve to absorb forced flow, it is time-varying, and it is riskiest when it pays most.

### Cited Findings
- Nagel (2012), "Evaporating Liquidity", Review of Financial Studies 25(7):2005-2039: returns of short-term reversal strategies in US equities proxy the returns from liquidity provision; expected return from liquidity provision is strongly time-varying and highly predictable with the VIX; expected returns and conditional Sharpe ratios rose "enormously" with the VIX during 2007-09; even industry-portfolio reversal strategies (weak unconditionally) earn high returns when VIX is high; interpretation: withdrawal of liquidity supply by constrained intermediaries — [NBER w17653](https://nber.org/papers/w17653); [PDF](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17653.pdf)
- Consistent micro-evidence in FX: rates reverse more often at take-profit clusters (round numbers) because those orders are negative-feedback (liquidity-providing) trades, even though they carry no information — [Osler SR150](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr150.pdf)
- Theory link: liquidity providers' capital constraints create liquidity spirals (Brunnermeier & Pedersen 2009) — [Princeton](https://markus.scholar.princeton.edu/document/98)

### Inferences
- A plausible, evidence-based behavior for COLLAPSAR is "fading" extreme short-term moves when volatility is high, but with the warning that this is when losses are also largest (spirals, gaps). With FTMO's -5 %/day wall, position size must shrink as volatility rises.
- This is the opposite of the SMC claim that the "smart money" engineers sweeps: in the academic view, reversal after forced selling is a compensation for risk-bearing, not evidence of manipulation.

### Gaps
- Nagel's numeric effect sizes (returns per unit of VIX, Sharpe ratios) were not retrieved.
- No retrieved study measures liquidity-provision reversal returns specifically on FX, gold, oil or crypto at 15-min to weekly horizons.

## 7. Retail "Smart Money Concepts" / ICT claims vs evidence

### Takeaway
No peer-reviewed study supports SMC/ICT constructs (order blocks, fair value gaps, liquidity sweeps, kill zones, "institutional levels") as profitable or even as measurable phenomena. The parts that have academic cousins (orders cluster near round numbers; breaks of those levels accelerate briefly; reversals after forced flow) show SMALL effects, are tied to public round numbers rather than to specific candles, and in the case of "stop hunt then reverse" point the other way on average (break → short continuation). Performance claims (fill rates, FTMO payouts) are unsourced marketing or survivor anecdotes.

### Cited Findings
- Origin: SMC derives from Michael Huddleston ("ICT", Inner Circle Trader), developed in the 2010s and later rebranded "SMC" — [AlphaEx Capital explainer](https://www.alphaexcapital.com/smart-money-concepts/order-blocks-explained) (commercial education site)
- The same source concedes: an order block is "methodology and lore, not measured fact"; "there is no peer-reviewed study showing that the last opposite candle marks where institutions placed real orders"; "80 % fill rate"-type statistics are "unsourced lore"; "no verified backtest of ICT order blocks exists in the academic or tier-1 literature"; the closest anchor is Osler's stop-loss clustering research — [AlphaEx Capital](https://www.alphaexcapital.com/smart-money-concepts/order-blocks-explained)
- The only academic-style document found is a 2021 bachelor's thesis (Theseus, Finnish university of applied sciences) building an SMC strategy for new traders; not peer-reviewed — [Theseus](https://www.theseus.fi/handle/10024/505690)
- Marketing anecdote: a trader "Dariusz" reportedly earned $1,206,225 on FTMO "using the SMC method" (2022) — reported in podcast/marketing material surfaced by search, unverifiable, single survivor — [Audible podcast listing](https://www.audible.fr/podcast/Smart-Money-Concepts-Profitability-and-Real-World-Success/B0CN2PSJRH) (UNVERIFIABLE)
- Many sites sell "SMC/ICT backtesting" tools but publish no audited out-of-sample results — [Backtrex](https://backtrex.com/en/blog/ict-order-block-backtest-strategy) (commercial)

**Claim-by-claim confrontation (retail claim → closest academic evidence)**

| Retail claim | Closest evidence | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| "Liquidity pools" of stops sit above highs / below lows | Osler: stop-loss buys cluster just ABOVE round numbers, sells just BELOW (14.4 % vs 7.4 % of stop buys at 01-10 vs 90-99) — [SR125](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr125.pdf) | Partly established, but for ROUND NUMBERS in one bank's book (1999-2000); clustering beyond swing highs/lows not tested |
| "Stop hunts / liquidity sweeps": price spikes through stops then reverses | Osler: crossing a round number is followed on average by FASTER continuation (e.g., +0.007 % extra over 15 min, significant for hours) — [SR150](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr150.pdf) | Average evidence contradicts "sweep-then-reverse"; deliberate hunting by institutions is unverified (dealers do see their own stop orders, Osler SR125, but no study shows systematic hunting) |
| "Institutional levels" (00/50, quarter levels) | Strong clustering of orders and of published S/R at 0/5 endings; 25/75 NOT special in Osler's data; bounce edge ≈ +4-5 pp over a ~55 % base at 15 min — [Osler 2000](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/epr/00v06n2/0007osle.pdf); [SR125](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr125.pdf) | Established but small; "quarter levels" not supported by Osler's FX order data |
| "Supply/demand zones" hold | Published S/R beats random levels (60.8 % vs 56.2 % bounce), weaker in normal volatility, little gain from consensus — [Osler 2000](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/epr/00v06n2/0007osle.pdf) | Weak statistical effect, not shown to be profitable after costs |
| "Order blocks" (last opposite candle before an impulse) mark institutional orders | No study found — [AlphaEx Capital](https://www.alphaexcapital.com/smart-money-concepts/order-blocks-explained) | Unverifiable / no evidence |
| "Fair value gaps" get filled | No peer-reviewed study found | Unverifiable |
| Candles reveal "institutional order flow" | Real order flow (Evans & Lyons) is signed interdealer data invisible in OHLC; retail volume is tick count — [NBER w7317](https://nber.org/papers/w7317) | Not supported: OHLC cannot reveal signed institutional flow |
| Round numbers matter in crypto | Bitcoin prices cluster at 00 (>10 %) but no significant return pattern after round numbers — [Urquhart 2017](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ecolet/v159y2017icp145-148.html) | Clustering yes, tradable effect no |
| Liquidation cascades in crypto can be anticipated | Price warnings in 5/7 cascades, none in shock-driven ones; consistent precursor only in taker order-flow variance — [arXiv 2607.27070](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27070) | Partly; not reliably from price alone (preprint) |

### Inferences
- SMC vocabulary repackages genuine but small microstructure facts (order clustering at round numbers, stop cascades, liquidity provision) into deterministic narratives with discretionary, non-falsifiable definitions (which candle is "the" order block is decided after the fact). That makes them untestable as stated, and any edge, if it exists, would have to be rediscovered from raw prices by a learning system.
- For COLLAPSAR, the evidence supports: (1) showing round-number distance and recent swing highs/lows as raw information, (2) expecting tiny effects that may vanish after costs, (3) treating stops placed just beyond obvious levels as exposed to worse fills, (4) not hard-coding any SMC rule (consistent with the project's "information, not trigger" principle).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed test of SMC/ICT constructs (order blocks, FVG, kill zones, break of structure) was found in this search; absence of evidence here is from a limited search pass (web search, not a systematic database review of SSRN/Scopus).
- Whether retail CFD brokers (B-book) or prop firms' price feeds produce "stop hunting" behavior (wicks not present in interbank prices) is unverified; no data found.
- Claims about intraday session timing ("kill zones") were not checked against the intraday seasonality literature in this note.
